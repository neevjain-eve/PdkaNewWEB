Background

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) received assent on 11 August 2023. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 were notified on 14 November 2025. According to the Press Information Bureau, the Rules provide for an eighteen-month phased compliance timeline. The Act applies to the processing of digital personal data, and the organisations that decide why and how the data is processed are called Data Fiduciaries.

What the Rules describe

  • Notice and consent: Data Fiduciaries are to give clear, standalone notices that state the specific purpose for which personal data is collected and used.
  • Rights of individuals: individuals (Data Principals) can ask for access, correction, updating and erasure of their personal data, and can nominate another person to exercise these rights. Requests are to be answered within a maximum of ninety days.
  • Designated officer: Data Fiduciaries are to publish how a designated officer or Data Protection Officer can be reached.
  • Children and persons with disabilities: verifiable consent of a parent or lawful guardian is required before a child's data is processed, with limited exemptions such as healthcare, education and real-time safety. Where a person cannot make legal decisions even with support, consent comes from a lawful guardian.
  • Personal data breaches: affected individuals are to be told without delay, in plain language, about the nature of the breach, its likely consequences, the steps taken and where to seek help. The Data Protection Board is also to be informed as the Rules prescribe.
  • Consent Managers: they must be companies based in India.
  • Significant Data Fiduciaries: these face additional duties, including independent audits, impact assessments and compliance with government-specified restrictions on certain categories of data.

Maximum penalties

The Act sets the following upper limits for penalties imposed by the Data Protection Board:

  • Failure to maintain reasonable security safeguards: up to ₹250 crore.
  • Failure to notify the Board or affected individuals of a personal data breach: up to ₹200 crore.
  • Failure to meet obligations relating to children: up to ₹200 crore.
  • Any other violation of the Act or the Rules: up to ₹50 crore.

Appeals against decisions of the Data Protection Board lie with the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT).

Practical points

  • Organisations commonly start by listing the personal data they hold, the purposes for which it is used, who receives it and how long it is kept.
  • Consent notices, systems for handling requests from individuals, and breach response procedures are the areas the Rules address most directly.
  • Contracts with vendors that process personal data on an organisation's behalf are a further area to review.
  • The commencement date of each provision should be checked in the Rules and in notifications of the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology.
